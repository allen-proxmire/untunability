# Reading: "A tiny glitch in time" (Bortolotti, Curceanu, Diósi, Manti, Piscicchia)

*2026-09-14. Ledger: C204, C205.*

**The article:** ScienceDaily, "Physicists just found a tiny glitch in time itself" (11 September 2026), from FQXi.

**The paper:** "Fundamental limits on clock precision from spacetime uncertainty in quantum collapse models", *Physical Review Research* 7, 043166 (2025), arXiv:2504.06109. The paper's text was read directly.

## What they did, in plain words

**1. They started from "collapse models".** These are proposals that quantum patterns collapse *on their own*, a little at a time, everywhere, without needing a measurement. The two best-known are:
- **Diósi–Penrose (DP),** where gravity sets the collapse strength;
- **Continuous Spontaneous Localization (CSL),** which has two adjustable numbers.

In both, nature is constantly and faintly "measuring" where mass is.

**2. They noticed the collapse noise behaves like a jittery gravity field.** The random kicks that cause collapse can be read as tiny random ups and downs in the Newtonian gravitational potential. This was already known for DP; **their new step was showing it for CSL too.**

**3. Then they used a basic fact from Einstein.** Clocks run slower where the gravitational potential is deeper. If the potential jitters randomly, a clock's rate jitters too, and its accumulated time drifts like a random walk.
- **The uncertainty grows like the square root of elapsed time:** Δt = √(τ t).
- **It is largest for clocks no bigger than the collapse model's "blur size" σ** (about 10⁻⁷ m for CSL, 10⁻⁹ m for DP).
- **Bigger clocks average the jitter away.**

**4. They put in numbers.** For the most sensitive clock size:

| model | clock-time uncertainty after 1 year |
|---|---|
| **CSL** (standard settings) | about **10⁻²⁸ s** (10⁻³¹ to 10⁻²⁶ s across the collapse rates experiments still allow) |
| **DP** | about **10⁻³¹ s** |
| **Best optical lattice clocks today** | about 10⁻¹⁷ s × √(seconds), roughly **6 × 10⁻¹⁴ s over a year** |

**So the effect is about 14 to 17 orders of magnitude below what the best clocks could see.**

## What it means

- **The headline "a glitch in time" is conditional.** The paper doesn't find a glitch. It shows that **if** collapse models are right, time must have an unavoidable, unimaginably small jitter.
- **It's a consistency result, not a discovery.** No experiment was done, and nothing was measured.
- **Its interest** is the link it draws between collapse, gravity and time, all in one mechanism.

## Why it's close to ED (C205)

The mechanism is almost ED's own picture, run backwards:
- **In ED, clocks slow with the used budget U** (RD16, RD35, RD36). U is set by where mass is committed. **If U jittered, ED's clocks would jitter in exactly this way.**
- **The difference is what makes things jitter.** These collapse models have **spontaneous** collapses happening everywhere, all the time. **ED rejected spontaneous draws (RD26):** draws happen only when a mark can't be brought back (RD28). So ED doesn't predict this universal background jitter. Any jitter in ED would come from actual interactions, the way ordinary matter moving around changes gravity.
- **That makes it a clean point of difference in principle.** A universal clock jitter with no ordinary cause would favour spontaneous collapse over ED's option D (compare test T11). At 10⁻²⁸ s per year it can't be tested now or in the foreseeable future.
- **One more parallel:** their result that bigger clocks average the jitter away is like ED's clock-tick rate being averaged over the loci a pattern covers (RD18).
